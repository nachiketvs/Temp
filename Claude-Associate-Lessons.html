How a question is built, and how to read it

Almost every item is a short scenario followed by a question and four options. The scenario usually contains one decisive detail — a regulation, a person's employment, "customer-facing", "nobody is waiting", "confidential", "every time" — and the options are written so that two or three of them are things a reasonable person might do. Only one is best.

An exam-style item, dissected

A marketing analyst uses Claude to summarise 300 customer reviews for a product decision. The summary is well written and every point in it checks out against the reviews. The product team later discovers a major complaint theme was never mentioned. Which failure occurred, and what would most likely have prevented it?

  1. Hallucination; instruct Claude to use only the supplied reviews and to say when something is not covered
  2. Omission; ask Claude to list the themes it found and the approximate count behind each, then check coverage
  3. Source bias; give Claude a larger and more representative sample of reviews before summarising
  4. Framing bias; rephrase the request neutrally so the summary does not favour any particular theme

How to read it. The decisive detail is "every point checks out" — that rules out hallucination (A) by definition. Nothing says the sample was skewed (C) or the question was leading (D). What happened is that something true was left out: omission. And the prevention has to make coverage visible — counts per theme let you see what is small and what is missing. B. Notice that A, C and D are all real techniques from these lessons; they are wrong only because they answer a different question.

The tells you should not rely on. The real exam does not make the longest option correct, does not put the answer in the same position, and does not use silly distractors. The questions in these lessons are written the same way. If you find yourself picking by length or by "the one with the most words I recognise", stop and find the decisive detail.

Unit 0Assumed knowledge · every domain rests on it

Foundations — what the exam assumes you already know

The exam never explains what a token is, or why Claude "forgets", or what the difference between a Project and memory is. It assumes you know. These five lessons make sure you do.

Lesson 0.1

Claude the model versus Claude the product

Why this matters. Roughly a third of Selection, Configuration and Troubleshooting questions turn on whether a problem belongs to the model (the thing that generates text) or the product (the software around it). Diagnose the wrong layer and you pick the wrong fix — and the wrong fix is always one of the options.

The idea

When people say "Claude", they can mean two quite different things.

The first is the model: a neural network with a name like Claude Sonnet 5. Text goes in, text comes out. It has no memory of anything that happened before this request, no files, no internet, no ability to click a button. If you called it twice with the same input you would be starting from zero both times. Think of it as an extraordinarily well-read consultant who has total amnesia between meetings and cannot leave the room.

The second is the product: the software Anthropic wraps around that model so it becomes useful. The claude.ai chat interface. Projects that hold your documents. Memory that carries context between conversations. Connectors that reach your Google Drive. Cowork that works through the files on your laptop. Each of these gives the amnesiac consultant something — a filing cabinet, a notebook, a phone line — that the model itself does not have.

The model is the engine. The products are the vehicles. The same engine sits inside every vehicle.

Worked examples

Example 1 — "Claude forgot my instructions"

A colleague says: "Last week I told Claude to always use British spelling and to sign off emails with our legal footer. Today it has forgotten. The model is unreliable."

Wrong diagnosis "The model is unreliable" — and the fix that follows is "use a more capable model". It will not help. No model retains anything between requests; a bigger one just forgets more expensively.

Right diagnosis This is a product question about persistence. The fix is to put the two rules into a Project's custom instructions, where they are applied to every conversation in that Project, identically, forever. (Memory, which we meet in Lesson 0.3, might also pick them up — but memory is approximate and instructions are exact.)

Example 2 — "The contract analysis is shallow"

A lawyer has set up a Project with the firm's precedents, clear instructions, and the contract attached. The analysis of a genuinely tricky indemnity clause is still superficial.

Wrong diagnosis "Add more documents to the Project." The information is already there; adding more will not deepen the reasoning.

Right diagnosis This is a model question about capability. With the information side already clean, the lever is the model tier (or extended thinking). Lesson 0.4.

Example 3 — "Can Claude look at my CRM?"

The model cannot look at anything. But the product can, through a connector. When a question asks how Claude "accesses" a system, it is always asking about a product feature, never about the model.

The diagnostic question to ask every time

Is this about information and persistence, or about capability? Information and persistence problems — missing context, forgotten rules, no access to a system, stale facts — are product problems, fixed with Projects, memory, connectors, uploads and search. Capability problems — shallow reasoning on a hard task when the information is already present — are model problems, fixed with tier or thinking. Most problems are information problems, and those fixes are free.

How the exam asks it

You will rarely see the words "model versus product". You will see a scenario and four fixes, one of which upgrades the model, one of which configures the product, and two of which are plausible noise. The task is to spot which layer the symptom belongs to.

Lesson 0.2

Tokens, and the context window

Why this matters. Domain 3 explicitly lists "manage context limitations". Cost questions, "why is it slow" questions and "why did it fail on the big document" questions all come back to tokens. You do not need arithmetic; you need the intuition.

The idea

You read in words. A model reads in tokens — chunks of text a little smaller than a word. Common short words are one token; longer or rarer words are split into pieces; punctuation and spaces count too.

"cat"               ->  cat                          1 token
"unbelievable"      ->  un | believ | able           3 tokens
"Hello, world!"     ->  Hello | , |  world | !       4 tokens
"₹50,000"           ->  ₹ | 50 | , | 000             4 tokens

Why should you care about a unit you never see? Because everything is measured in tokens: how much you can send, how much is generated, what it costs, and how long it takes.

The rule of thumb for English: one token is roughly 4 characters, or roughly ¾ of a word. So 1,000 tokens is about 750 words — a page and a half. A 40-page report is somewhere around 20,000–30,000 tokens. A 400-page manual is a few hundred thousand.

Three token concepts you must be able to tell apart

ConceptWhat it isWhy it matters to you
InputEverything sent to the model in one exchange: your instructions, the whole conversation so far, any attached files, Project knowledge that is pulled in.This is usually the bulk of what you pay for. Big attachments dominate it.
OutputWhat the model generates in reply.Priced roughly five times higher per token than input. Long answers are the expensive kind.
Context windowThe maximum input plus output that can exist in a single exchange. Current frontier models: 1 million tokens. Haiku: 200,000.Exceed it and the exchange cannot proceed. It does not quietly forget the start — the product either compacts the conversation or asks you to start afresh.

Worked examples

Example 1 — the multilingual surprise

A team estimates costs by testing on English support tickets. Production tickets are 60% Hindi and Tamil, with pasted error logs. Real costs come in 35% over the estimate.

Nothing is wrong. Non-English scripts, code and log output tokenize less efficiently: the same meaning takes more tokens. The estimate should have been made on representative documents. On the exam this appears as "why are costs higher than expected?" — and the answer is tokenization, not a pricing change.

Example 2 — "just paste the whole manual"

An operations manager wants to paste a 400-page procedures manual into every conversation "because the window is a million tokens, it fits".

Why it is a bad idea even though it fits Every message re-sends the manual, so every reply is slow and expensive. Worse, the useful five paragraphs are buried in four hundred pages, and answers get less precise, not more.

Better Put the manual in a Project's knowledge base so it is available without being re-pasted, and when asking a specific question, point at the relevant section: "Using the section on returns handling in the Procedures Manual, tell me..." The window is headroom, not a target.

Example 3 — the expensive habit

Two analysts ask the same question. One writes: "Give me a full, comprehensive, detailed explanation with all the background." The other writes: "In under 150 words, tell me the two things I need to decide today." The first answer costs several times more, takes longer, and the analyst reads only the last paragraph anyway. Output tokens are the expensive kind; ask for what you will actually read.

Mistakes people make

1Assuming "it fits" means "it is a good idea". Relevance beats volume.
2Estimating cost on English samples for a non-English workload.
3Treating a long conversation as free. Every turn re-sends everything before it (Lesson 0.3).
4Forgetting that Haiku's window is a fifth of the frontier tiers' when choosing a model for a huge document.
Lesson 0.3

Why Claude "forgets" — and the three ways it remembers

Why this matters. This is the most-misunderstood idea in the whole syllabus, and the product changed in 2026 so that older study material is now wrong. Configuration questions, Selection questions and several Troubleshooting questions hinge on it.

The idea: the model is stateless

The model itself remembers nothing between requests. When a chat seems to remember what you said ten messages ago, here is what is actually happening: the product sends the entire conversation so far, plus your new message, to the model every single time. The model reads it all afresh and replies. It is not remembering; it is re-reading.

Every turn re-sends everything before it. This one picture explains long-chat drift, why Projects exist, and why starting over is sometimes the best move.

So how does Claude remember anything? Three separate mechanisms

As of 2026 there are three kinds of persistence, and the exam expects you to know which is which, because they behave differently and the right fix depends on the difference.

MechanismWhat persistsHow exact is it?You control it by
1. Conversation historyEverything in this chat, re-sent each turnExact, but only within the chatStarting a new chat ends it. Near the limit, Claude may compact (summarise) the early part automatically.
2. Project knowledge and instructionsDocuments you uploaded or connected, and standing rules you wrote. Identical for every conversation in that Project.Exact and deliberate. The actual document, the actual rule, every time.Adding, editing, removing files and instructions. Nothing enters a Project unless someone puts it there.
3. MemorySummaries Claude derives from your past conversations: preferences, recurring context, things you asked it to remember. Available to all users since March 2026.Approximate. Claude decides what is worth keeping and stores a summary, not a transcript.Settings → Capabilities → Memory: view, edit, delete individual memories, or switch it off. Or just ask Claude to remember or forget something.

How memory is scoped — learn this precisely

Each Project has its own separate memory space. What Claude learns while you work inside "Client A" stays in Client A; it does not surface inside "Client B". Conversations outside any Project — ordinary chats, and since 25 August 2026 Cowork tasks too — feed one shared general memory. Memory is private to your account: teammates do not see it, and it is not used to train models unless you opt in.

Worked examples

Example 1 — the correction that did not stick

On Monday an analyst tells Claude, in an ordinary chat, "Our fiscal year starts in April, not January." On Thursday, in a different chat, Claude assumes a January year-start.

What happened: the Monday correction lived in conversation history (mechanism 1), which ended with that chat. Memory (mechanism 3) might have captured it — it often does — but memory is approximate and there is no guarantee. If the April year-start must be applied reliably, it belongs in a Project's custom instructions (mechanism 2): "Our fiscal year runs April to March. Interpret all quarters accordingly."

Example 2 — two clients, one consultant

A consultant keeps Client A and Client B in separate Projects. Inside Client A she mentions a confidential pricing strategy. Later, working in Client B, she worries Claude might mention it.

It will not. Project memory spaces are isolated. This is precisely why "one Project per client" is the recommended design — it aligns the memory boundary and the knowledge boundary with the confidentiality boundary.

Example 3 — what memory is good for

Memory shines at low-stakes continuity: that you prefer tables to prose, that you write in British English, that you are working on a product launch this quarter. Claude picks these up and generally tells you when it is drawing on them. What memory is not for: anything that must be applied identically every time, or that your teammates also need. That is what instructions are for.

The distinction the exam tests. Memory is derived and approximate. Project instructions and knowledge are deliberate and exact. When a scenario says a rule "must" apply, or must apply "for the whole team", or "every time", the answer is instructions or knowledge, not memory. An option that says "tell Claude once and it will remember" is the distractor.
Lesson 0.4

The models: tiers, trade-offs and the September 2026 line-up

Why this matters. Domain 3 says "differentiate Claude models" and "align selection with task requirements". You will not be asked to compute a bill. You will be asked which tier fits a scenario, and which change moves cost in which direction.

The idea

Anthropic ships models in tiers. Model names change with each generation; the shape of the trade-off never does: more capability costs more per token and is usually slower. The skill is matching the tier to what the task actually needs — not to what feels safest.

TierCurrent (Sep 2026)CharacterReach for it when
FlagshipClaude Fable 5.1 1 Sep 2026Anthropic's most capable generally-available model. Deepest reasoning; long-running autonomous work; heavy document, spreadsheet and slide work. Thinking always on.The hardest, highest-stakes tasks, where a wrong answer is expensive and nobody is waiting on a stopwatch.
OpusClaude Opus 5Very high capability; the standard choice for complex analysis and agentic work.Genuinely hard problems, ambiguity, long multi-step tasks.
SonnetClaude Sonnet 5The balanced workhorse: strong at most things, meaningfully cheaper than Opus.The sensible default for day-to-day professional work.
HaikuClaude Haiku 4.5Fastest and cheapest; smaller 200K window.Simple, repetitive, high-volume tasks: sorting, tagging, extracting, short summaries.
Relative cost is what you need. Each step down the table is roughly two to five times cheaper per token. Output tokens cost several times more than input at every tier. "Which change lowers cost?" is answerable from those two facts.

Two controls you should recognise by name

Extended (adaptive) thinkingThe model can reason internally before answering. On current models this is adaptive — Claude decides how much to think — and depth is steered with an effort setting (low to max). More thinking helps hard problems and costs more; on tasks that were never hard it adds cost and nothing else.
Research modeClaude runs a multi-step investigation — web search, your connected tools, your documents — and returns a cited report. Slower and more thorough than one search-enabled answer.

Worked examples

Example 1 — 80,000 survey comments a day

A CX team must tag every free-text survey comment as positive, neutral or negative. Same three labels, all day, every day.

The safe-feeling choice "Use the flagship, we want it to be accurate." At 80,000 items a day that is an enormous bill for a task that never needed deep reasoning.

The right approach Start with Haiku. Check its labels against a sample the team has tagged by hand. If accuracy is fine — and for three-way sentiment it usually is — you are done. If it is not, step up one tier and check again. Measure, then choose.

Example 2 — the one contract that matters

A CFO needs a careful read of a 90-page acquisition agreement, once, by Friday. No volume; high stakes; time to spare.

Here the cost of a wrong answer dominates everything. Use the most capable model, with extended thinking on, and give it the whole agreement and a precise brief. Saving a few dollars on this task would be a false economy.

Example 3 — both at once

A support desk classifies 50,000 tickets a day into eight queues. About 3% are messy escalations that need real judgement.

Neither "cheap for all" nor "capable for all" is right. Route: Haiku classifies everything; anything it flags as complex or low-confidence goes to Sonnet or Opus. You pay premium prices on 3% of traffic. When a scenario gives you high volume and a hard minority, look for the option that splits them.

Mistakes people make

1Starting cheap and hoping, with no measurement.
2Paying flagship prices for classification "to be safe".
3Upgrading the tier to fix a missing-information problem (Lesson 0.1).
4Enabling extended thinking on trivial tasks, adding cost for nothing.
5Inventing model names. Current names are simply Claude Opus 5, Claude Sonnet 5, Claude Haiku 4.5, Claude Fable 5.1.

Unit 1 · Domain 114% · ~8 questions

Prompting and Task Execution

Official task statements: create effective prompts; apply task decomposition; iterate for quality; adapt strategy by task type. This is the framework's Description competency. Four lessons, every technique shown on a real prompt.

Lesson 1.1

What a prompt has to contain, and why

Why this matters. Most Domain 1 questions show you a weak prompt and four ways to change it. To pick the right change you need to be able to name what is missing. This lesson gives you the six-part checklist and shows each part doing its job.

The idea: you are briefing a brilliant stranger

Imagine hiring a consultant who has read everything ever published, works at lightning speed, has never heard of your company, cannot ask you a clarifying question before starting, and will confidently hand you something no matter how vague your brief is. You would not say "do me a report". You would say what it is for, who reads it, how long it should be, what to use and not use, and what a good one looks like.

That is all a prompt is. Every technique in this unit is a way of briefing better. And the single most important fact about the model is this:

The model will always produce something. Its willingness to answer is not a signal that your prompt was good.

The six components

ComponentThe question it answersWhat goes wrong without it
RoleFrom whose expertise and perspective?Generic, unfocused register
TaskWhat exactly must be done?The model guesses the verb: "review" becomes "summarise"
ContextWhat background is needed to do it well?Output that could be about any company

Worked example — one request, three levels

Level 1 — the request most people send
Summarise this customer feedback.

[340 support tickets pasted]

What you get: a neutral paragraph of about 200 words saying customers have "a range of concerns including delivery times, product quality and customer service". True, and useless. The model had to guess the audience, the length and the purpose, and guessed "generic".

Level 2 — adding task, audience and format
I am preparing a quarterly product review for the VP of Product.
From these 340 support tickets, identify the five most frequent
complaint themes. For each theme give the theme name, an approximate
count of tickets, and one representative verbatim quote.
Output as a table.

[340 support tickets pasted]

What you get: a five-row table with counts and quotes. Now usable. But look at what can still go wrong: the "approximate count" for a rare theme might be a guess dressed as a number, and nothing tells the VP whether a theme is a product defect or a mismatch of expectations.

Level 3 — adding constraints and a visible-uncertainty rule
You are a product analyst preparing a quarterly review for the VP of
Product, who will decide which two problems the engineering team
fixes next quarter.

From the support tickets between the <tickets> tags, identify the
five most frequent complaint themes. For each theme:
  - theme name
  - approximate number of tickets (count them; do not estimate)
  - one representative verbatim quote, with its ticket number
  - whether it is a product defect or an expectation gap, in one line

Rules:
  - Use only the tickets provided. Do not add themes from general
    knowledge of similar products.
  - If a theme is supported by fewer than 10 tickets, mark it
    LOW-CONFIDENCE.
  - If fewer than five clear themes exist, list fewer. Do not pad.

Output as a table, followed by a two-sentence note on what the data
cannot tell us.

<tickets>
[340 support tickets]
</tickets>

What you get: a table the VP can act on, with the weak rows flagged, the source grounded, and permission to return fewer than five. Compare the three prompts: nothing in Level 3 is clever. It is simply complete.

The most examinable line in that prompt is mark it LOW-CONFIDENCE. It converts a hidden weakness in the output into a visible one that a reviewer can act on. Asking the model to expose its own uncertainty is the technique that connects this domain to the 21% Evaluation domain, and it appears on the paper in several forms.

Mistakes people make

1Mistaking a role for context. "You are an expert analyst" sets a register; it does not tell the model your product, your audience or your decision.
2Asking for length instead of purpose. "Under 300 words" says nothing about what belongs in those words. "For a CFO deciding whether to renew" does.
3Requesting a fixed number. "Give me the top five risks" from a document with two will get you five, three of them padding. Say "up to five, only those supported by the document".
4Leaving the source unmarked. Instructions and pasted material become one wall of text; the model may analyse your instructions. Lesson 1.2 fixes this with delimiters.

How the exam asks it

"Which single addition would most improve this prompt?" The options will each add one component. Identify which component the weak output is missing — generic means context/audience, wrong shape means format, invented facts means a grounding constraint — and choose that one. A bigger model is never the answer to a missing component.

Lesson 1.2

The techniques, each shown on a real prompt

Why this matters. The exam names these techniques — zero-shot, few-shot, chain of thought, decomposition, chaining, delimiters — and expects you to know what each looks like and when it is the right choice. This lesson shows each one working.

Zero-shot: describe the task, give no examples

What it looks like
Classify each of the following customer messages as one of:
COMPLAINT, QUESTION, PRAISE, or OTHER.
Reply with the message number and the label only.

1. "My order arrived two weeks late and the box was crushed."
2. "Do you ship to Singapore?"
3. "The new app update is brilliant, thank you."
4. "Please unsubscribe me from the newsletter."

Output: 1. COMPLAINT  2. QUESTION  3. PRAISE  4. OTHER

When it is enough: the task is common, the categories are obvious, and the format is easy to describe. Zero-shot is the default; you escalate only when it fails.

Few-shot: show two to five worked examples

Label: COMPLAINT

Now classify:
1. "Why does the site keep logging me out?"
2. "What are your opening hours?"

Output: 1. COMPLAINT  2. QUESTION — and it will stay consistent across thousands of messages, because it has seen the boundary rather than read about it.

When to use it: the output format is idiosyncratic, or the judgement is subtle and easier to show than to describe. This is the strongest single quality lever a non-technical user has, and the exam's signal phrase is "hard to describe in words".

Chain of thought: reasoning before the conclusion

What it looks like
A supplier offers a 4% discount for payment within 10 days instead of
our usual 45 days. Our cost of capital is 9% a year.

Before giving a recommendation, work through the annualised value of
the discount step by step, then state whether we should take it and
why.

Output (abridged): "Step 1: paying 35 days early... Step 2: 4% over 35 days annualises to roughly 42%... Step 3: 42% exceeds our 9% cost of capital... Recommendation: take the discount."

Why it helps: the reasoning informs the answer instead of rationalising one already stated, and if a step is wrong you can see which. On current models, extended thinking does much of this internally; the technique still matters for making the reasoning visible to you. Use it for analysis, trade-offs, anything where the path matters.

Delimiters: separating your instructions from the material

The failure it prevents

An HR partner pastes a twelve-page policy and asks what it says about leave. Claude answers from general knowledge of employment law instead. Nothing marked the policy as "the thing to read"; it and the question were one undifferentiated block.

Answer the question using only the policy between the <policy> tags.
If the policy does not address the question, say so.

<policy>
[twelve pages of policy text]
</policy>

Question: What does this policy say about carrying unused leave into
the next year?

Why it matters twice: delimiters improve quality and are the first defence against prompt injection (Unit 6), because they let you say "text inside these tags is data, not instructions".

Format skeleton: show the exact shape you want

What it looks like
For each clause you flag, output exactly this block:

Clause: [clause number]
Quoted text: "[verbatim, no paraphrase]"
Risk: HIGH | MEDIUM | LOW
Why: [one sentence]

Do not add anything outside these blocks.

Why it matters: without it, the shape changes between runs. With it, output can feed a template or a downstream process reliably.

Task decomposition and prompt chaining

The failure they prevent

Asked for a 40-page competitive analysis in one go, the model produces something thin that drifts by page fifteen and repeats itself by page thirty. Long single generations decay.

Decomposition: split the job into stages you check between.

Stage 1 prompt:
  Propose an outline for a 40-page competitive analysis of [market],
  with section headings and two lines on what each covers. Do not
  write the sections yet.

[Human reviews and edits the outline]

Stage 2 prompt (repeated per section):
  Using the approved outline below, write Section 3 (Pricing Models)
  in about 900 words. Use only the sources between the <sources> tags.
  <outline>...</outline>
  <sources>...</sources>

Chaining is decomposition where each stage's checked output becomes the next stage's input: extract the facts → check them → draft from the checked facts → edit the draft. The human gate between stages is the point: you correct direction early instead of after forty pages.

Lesson 1.3

Adapting the prompt to the type of task

Why this matters. The fourth task statement is "adapt strategies by task type". The same six components apply to everything, but which ones carry the weight changes. The exam tests whether you know that an extraction prompt and a drafting prompt should look different.

Extraction — pull facts out; want zero creativity

From each invoice between the <invoice> tags, extract:
  invoice_number, supplier_name, invoice_date (ISO format), total_amount, currency.

Rules: Copy values exactly as they appear. Do not correct, infer or
normalise anything except the date format. If a field is not present,
write NOT FOUND. Output one line per invoice, fields separated by |.

What carries the weight: delimiters, "copy exactly", an explicit NOT FOUND path, a rigid format. The one thing you do not want is initiative.

Summarisation — the audience and the decision decide the content

Summarise the attached 90-page audit report for the board, which will
decide whether to commission a follow-up audit. Lead with any finding
that requires a decision. Keep background to two lines. Under 400 words.

What carries the weight: audience and purpose. Compare "summarise this in 400 words" — same length, no idea what belongs in it.

Classification — a closed set with a catch-all and examples

Assign each ticket exactly one category from: BILLING, TECHNICAL, ACCOUNT,
COMPLIANCE, OTHER. Use OTHER when a ticket does not clearly fit, or when
you are unsure. If a ticket spans two categories, choose the one the
customer most needs resolved.

[three examples per category, including borderline ones]

What carries the weight: every category defined, a catch-all so ambiguity has a safe home, a tie-break rule, examples.

Drafting and creative work — voice, audience, examples, iteration

Draft a 150-word customer email announcing a two-week delay to their order.
Tone: direct, warm, no corporate apology language ("we apologise for any
inconvenience"). Audience: a customer who has already complained once.
Include the new date, a specific gesture (free express shipping on the
next order), and a named contact.

Here are two emails in our house voice for reference:
<example>...</example>
<example>...</example>

What carries the weight: voice, audience, what to avoid, house-style examples. Expect to iterate; nobody nails a customer email in one shot.

Analysis and reasoning — reasoning first, counter-case, falsifiability

Should we move our support desk from phone-first to chat-first?
Work through the case for and against, using the data between the
<data> tags. Then give a recommendation, the strongest argument
against it, and what evidence would change your mind.

What carries the weight: reasoning before conclusion, an explicit request for the counter-case (which also guards against framing bias, Unit 2), and "what would change your mind", which makes the analysis auditable.

Research — grounding, citations, date range

Using web search, summarise regulatory changes to data-residency rules in
India announced in the last 90 days. Cite every claim with its source and
date. Flag any conflict between sources. Do not include changes announced
earlier than 90 days ago.

What carries the weight: enabling search (a training cutoff is a hard boundary), citations, an explicit date window.

Translation and rewriting — register and preservation

Translate the policy below into Hindi for internal staff. Formal register.
Keep all headings, numbering and product names exactly as in the source.
Do not add explanations.

The pattern

Extraction and classification want constraint (no initiative). Drafting wants voice and examples (initiative, guided). Analysis wants visible reasoning and the counter-case. Research wants grounding and citations. Summarisation wants audience and purpose. Same six components; different emphasis.

Output Evaluation and Validation

Official task statements: evaluate accuracy and completeness; identify hallucinations and biases; apply fact-checking; determine when human review is needed; edit and format outputs to professional standards. This is Discernment. Five lessons; if you are short of time, spend it here.

Lesson 2.1

Why a fluent answer is not a correct answer

Why this matters. Every other domain produces output. This one decides whether the output can be used. It is a fifth of the exam because it is where real-world harm happens: a confident, well-formatted, wrong document that nobody checked.

The idea

A language model produces the most plausible continuation of the text so far. Plausibility and truth are strongly correlated — that is why the model is useful at all — but they are not the same thing, and the gap between them is where the danger lives.

When the model has solid grounding — the fact is in the document you gave it, or is thoroughly represented in what it learned — the plausible answer is the true one. When it does not have grounding, the most plausible-looking answer is a confidently phrased, well-formatted, specific, invented one. And here is the critical point:

A fabricated answer looks identical to a correct one. Same confident tone, same clean formatting, same authoritative detail.

This is what people mean by hallucination. Not "the model lies" — it has no intent. It fills a gap with the most plausible shape, and specifics are more plausible-looking than vagueness. So hallucination clusters around exactly the things that look most trustworthy: citations, statistics, names, dates, case numbers, product features, URLs.

Consequence for how you read output: detail is a risk marker, not a reliability marker. The more specific and checkable a claim is, the more it needs checking.

Worked examples

Example 1 — the perfect bibliography

A researcher asks for "key academic papers on remote-work productivity since 2020, with citations". The output lists eight papers: authors, journal, year, volume, page numbers, a one-line summary of each. It looks like a page from a literature review.

Two of the eight do not exist. Their authors are real researchers in the field; the journals are real; the titles are exactly the kind of title such a paper would have. Nothing on the page distinguishes the six real papers from the two invented ones. The only way to know is to look each one up.

What the exam wants you to conclude: citations are the highest-risk output category, every one must be checked against an external source, and formatting quality tells you nothing.

Example 2 — the confident statistic

A market brief says: "According to a 2025 Deloitte survey, 63% of mid-market CFOs plan to increase AI spend." The sentence has a named source, a year, a precise percentage and a defined population. It is exactly what a real statistic looks like.

Correct handling: find the Deloitte publication. If it exists and says 63%, keep it. If you cannot find it, the claim cannot be used — remove it or flag it as unverified. "Ask Claude if it is sure" is not a step; see Lesson 2.3.

Example 3 — the feature that is not there

A customer-facing FAQ, drafted by Claude from general knowledge of the product, says the plan "supports up to 500 users". The real limit is 250. The sentence is fluent and specific, and it went live because it read well.

Three things would have prevented it: grounding the draft in the product specification rather than general knowledge; instructing Claude to flag any claim not in the spec; and a human checking every specific before publication. Notice all three appear as separate techniques later in this unit.

Mistakes people make

1Trusting an output because it is detailed. Detail is where fabrication hides.
2Treating "it sounds right" as a check. Sounding right is what the model optimises for.
3Assuming a named source proves the claim. A named source is a plausibility feature until you have opened it.
Lesson 2.2

The four failure modes, and how each is caught

Why this matters. The exam names the failure in a scenario and asks what happened, or asks what would have prevented it. You need to tell hallucination from inference from omission from bias, because each is caught differently and the wrong diagnosis leads to the wrong fix.
FailureWhat it looks likeHow you catch it
HallucinationA fact, citation, statistic, quote, case or feature that does not exist, stated plainly.Check every specific, checkable claim against a source.
Ungrounded inferenceThe source is real, but the conclusion drawn from it does not follow. A leap, presented as if the source made it.For each claim ask: "which sentence in the source supports this?"
OmissionEverything present is true; something material is missing — the caveat, the exception, the minority view, the sample size.The hardest, because nothing on the page is wrong. Ask "what would an expert expect to see here that is not here?"

Worked examples — the same contract, four failures

The source

A vendor contract says, in clause 14: "Either party may terminate on 90 days' written notice. Termination within the first 12 months incurs a fee equal to three months' charges." An analyst asks Claude to summarise the exit terms.

Hallucination

"Either party may terminate on 90 days' notice, subject to a 12-month minimum term and a £15,000 early-exit fee."

The £15,000 figure appears nowhere in the contract. It is invented, plausible, and precisely the kind of thing that ends up in a board paper. Caught by checking the specific against the clause.

Ungrounded inference

"The contract locks us in for 12 months with no realistic exit."

Every word is anchored to something real, but the conclusion does not follow: there is an exit, with a fee. The leap from "fee" to "no realistic exit" is the model's, presented as the contract's. Caught by asking which sentence says "no realistic exit". None does.

Omission

"Either party may terminate on 90 days' written notice."

True. Complete? No — the early-termination fee is missing, and that is the part the CFO needed. This summary passes every accuracy check and still causes a bad decision. Caught only by a completeness pass: "what else does clause 14 say?"

Bias (framing)

The analyst had asked: "Explain why these exit terms are favourable to us." The summary dutifully explains why they are favourable and omits the fee. Here the prompt caused the skew. Caught by noticing the leading question.

Omission is the one candidates miss. Any scenario of the shape "the summary was accurate but the decision was wrong" is an omission question. Accuracy of what is present is not completeness, and they are checked by different questions: accuracy by "is this claim supported?", completeness by "what is missing?"
Grounding reduces hallucination; it does not eliminate inference or omission. Giving Claude the document makes verification fast — the claim is either in the clause or it is not — but the model can still mis-read a figure, combine two numbers, draw a conclusion the document does not support, or leave out the part that mattered.
Lesson 2.3

A two-minute validation routine, and the self-check trap

Why this matters. "Apply fact-checking" is a task statement. The exam tests whether you have a method, and above all whether you know that asking the model to check itself is not fact-checking. That single distinction appears repeatedly.

The routine

1Verifiability sweep. Read the output once, underlining every specific claim: numbers, names, dates, citations, quotes, regulations, product capabilities, prices. Those are the checkable items. Everything else is interpretation or general principle.
2Grounding check. For each underlined item, ask: is this in a source I supplied, or did the model produce it from its own recall? Recalled specifics are the danger zone. Supplied specifics are quick to verify.
3Independent verification. Check recalled specifics against a real source: the document, the database, the publisher, the regulator's site. Not by asking the model.
4Completeness pass. What would an expert reader expect that is not here?
5Consequence sizing. If this claim were wrong, what happens? This sets how much verification is proportionate (Lesson 2.5).
6Accountability. Who is named as responsible for this output once it leaves your hands?

Worked example — the routine on a real output

The output (a supplier due-diligence note, drafted from an uploaded proposal plus general knowledge)

"Acme Logistics (Companies House no. 08123456) has traded since 2011 and reported revenue of £42m in FY2025. The firm holds ISO 9001 and is working towards ISO 27001. It suffered a publicised data breach in March 2024 affecting around 18,000 customer records. Culturally the team seems collaborative and responsive. Their proposal offers a 4% volume discount above 10,000 units."

Step 1, underline the specifics: the company number, 2011, £42m, FY2025, ISO 9001, ISO 27001, March 2024, 18,000, 4%, 10,000 units.

Step 2, grounding: ISO status, the discount and the volume threshold are in the uploaded proposal — quick to check against it. The company number, founding year, revenue and the breach are not in the proposal. They came from recall. Highest risk.

Step 3, verify externally: company number and founding year against Companies House; revenue against filed accounts; the breach against news or the ICO register. Suppose the breach checks out but affected 1,800 records, not 18,000. A plausible, order-of-magnitude fabrication — exactly the type that reads convincingly.

Step 4, completeness: a due-diligence note with no mention of insurance, financial stability trend, or key-person risk is missing things an expert would expect.

Step 5, consequence: this note supports a £2m contract award. High stakes; every specific gets verified.

Step 6: the procurement lead signs it. Not "AI-generated"; her name.

Notice what was not worth checking: "culturally the team seems collaborative" is opinion, not a checkable claim. Skim the generalities; verify the specifics.

The self-check trap

"Ask Claude whether its answer is correct" is not verification. When you ask the model to check itself, it generates another plausible continuation — this time one that discusses the first. It has no privileged access to whether a cited case exists or a figure is right. A self-reported "confidence score" is itself generated text.

Self-critique is genuinely useful for internal properties: structure, flow, internal consistency, completeness against a rubric, tone. It cannot establish external facts. Memorise the line: internal properties yes; external facts no.
Legitimate self-critique
Review the draft between the tags. Identify: any section where the
argument is weak or unsupported; any inconsistency between sections;
anything a sceptical board member would challenge; any place the tone
drifts from the rest. Do not verify facts — I will do that separately.

Every item is answerable by reading the text. Compare: "Confirm the revenue figure is correct" — not answerable from the text, so not a valid self-check.

The cheapest quality control there is

Instruct Claude, in the prompt or as a standing Project instruction: "Mark any claim you cannot support from the supplied sources with [UNVERIFIED], and mark any conclusion you are inferring rather than reading with [INFERRED]." This does not make the output accurate. It makes the weak parts visible, so a reviewer can go straight to them. It costs nothing, works for non-technical users, and appears on the exam in several disguises.
Lesson 2.4

Bias — the three shapes the exam means

Why this matters. "Identify biases" is a task statement. The exam does not mean bias in the political sense; it means systematic skew that distorts a decision or disadvantages a group. Three shapes recur, and two of them are caused by the person prompting.

Shape 1 — Representation bias: who is depicted

Create five example customer personas for our budgeting app.

Output: five personas, all men aged 35–50, all salaried, all urban. The app's actual users are 58% women, a third under 30, many self-employed. The personas will steer the product toward the wrong people.

Not hallucination — personas are meant to be invented. The failure is the skew. Fix: supply the real distribution and ask for personas that reflect it.

Create five personas reflecting our user base: 58% women, one third under
30, 40% self-employed, a quarter outside major cities. Vary income and
financial confidence. Do not default to a single demographic.

Shape 2 — Framing bias: the question caused it

Why is our new returns policy better for customers?

Output: a persuasive case for the policy. No drawbacks. The prompt asked for a case, not an assessment; the model delivered what was asked. The prompt caused the bias.

Assess our new returns policy from the customer's perspective. Where is
it better than the old one, where worse? Which customer groups gain and
which lose? End with the strongest objection a consumer advocate would raise.

This is the version most likely to appear as a scenario, because the fix is in the user's hands and the distractor ("the model is biased; use a better one") is tempting.

Shape 3 — Source bias: the input was skewed

A team gives Claude 400 support tickets and asks for a sentiment analysis. The result: customers are "broadly dissatisfied". The tickets were all complaints — nobody opens a ticket to say things are fine. The output faithfully reproduces a skewed sample, and reads as balanced because it is well written.

Fix upstream: a representative sample. Mitigate in the prompt: declare the sample.

These 400 tickets are complaints only; they do not represent customers
who did not contact support. Analyse the themes, and state this
limitation explicitly in your conclusions.

The universal countermeasure

Ask for the counter-case. "Now argue the opposite." "What would a critic say?" "Which perspectives are missing from the sources I gave you?" A model asked a leading question gives a one-sided answer; a model asked for both sides gives both. And in hiring, lending, medical and legal contexts, add: "Which groups could this disadvantage?"
Lesson 2.5

Proportionate review, mandatory human review, and editing to standard

Why this matters. Two task statements live here: "determine when human review is needed" and "edit and format outputs to professional standards". The first generates the exam's most predictable correct answers; the second is the one candidates skip because it sounds mundane.

Proportionality: the risk grid

Verifying everything at maximum depth is not diligence, it is paralysis. The professional skill is matching effort to two things: how bad it is if the output is wrong, and how easy it is to check.

Two axes, four behaviours. The vertical axis dominates. A regulated domain, an externally published artefact, or a decision about a person puts you in the top row no matter how easy verification looks.

When a human must review — the list to memorise

A qualified human must review, and remains accountable, whenever the output involves: medical, legal or financial advice; a decision about a specific person (hiring, promotion, credit, admission, discipline); anything regulated or filed with an authority; anything published externally under the organisation's name; anything safety-critical; and any output feeding an automated process with no downstream check. In these, "the AI checked it" is never the answer, and a stronger model never removes the requirement.

Over-reliance: the risk that grows because the system works

Six months in, a team has reviewed 500 AI-drafted summaries and almost all were fine. Reviewers now approve each one in about eight seconds. A colleague proposes dropping review altogether: "It is 98% accurate."

Two things are wrong. First, eight-second review is not review; the track record has eroded the check. Second, "98%" is not a risk assessment until you know what the 2% costs, which cases fall in it (errors are rarely random — they cluster in a case type), and whether eight-second glances would catch them anyway.

The control is structural: a written standard of what review means, periodic deep sampling, and audit — not "please be more careful". On the exam, an option that includes sampling, a review standard or audit beats one that just says "a human reviews it".

Editing to professional standard — the skipped task statement

An AI output is a draft. The blueprint explicitly expects you to turn it into a document. Concretely:

Before

"Certainly! Here is a summary of the audit report as you requested. I have followed your instructions to focus on findings requiring a decision. The report, which is comprehensive and detailed, identifies several areas... In conclusion, the audit highlights important issues. Let me know if you would like me to expand on any section!"

After

"Three findings require a board decision: (1)... (2)... (3)... Two further findings are being handled by management and need no decision."

What changed: the scaffolding ("Certainly!", the restated instructions, the closing offer) is gone; it is right-sized to what the reader needs; house style is applied; any specific that could not be verified was removed; and the analyst's name is on it. That last point is what Diligence means.

1Cut the scaffolding. Preambles, restated instructions, meta-commentary, closing offers.
2Apply house style. Terminology, spelling variant, headings, citation format.
3Right-size. Models default to more than readers want.
4Replace generic with specific where you have the specifics; remove specifics you could not verify.
5Own it. Once edited, it is your document.

Product and Model Selection

Official task statements: select appropriate features; differentiate Claude models; align selection with task requirements; manage context limitations. This is Delegation: the right vehicle, the right engine.

Lesson 3.1

Two decisions that get confused: surface and tier

Why this matters. Selection questions almost always contain one option that upgrades the model and one that changes the surface or configuration. Only one fits the symptom. This lesson makes the distinction automatic.

The idea

Decision 1 — which surface?

Chat? A Project? Cowork? A connector? Research mode? Claude in Excel? This is about where the work happens and what Claude can reach. It answers information and access problems.

Decision 2 — which model tier?

Fable, Opus, Sonnet or Haiku, and whether to enable extended thinking. This is about how hard the thinking is and what it costs. It answers capability problems.

Worked examples

Example 1 — wrong product names

Claude keeps calling the company's internal tools by the wrong names. A colleague suggests upgrading to Opus.

It will not help. Your internal naming is in no model's training data; a more capable model invents names more convincingly. This is an information problem: supply the glossary — as an attachment, or better, in the Project's knowledge with an instruction to use it.

Example 2 — the shallow analysis

The Project has the precedents, the brief is precise, the contract is attached. The analysis of a genuinely knotty clause is still superficial.

Information is already clean. This is the one situation where the tier lever is right: enable extended thinking, or move up a tier, and ask for reasoning before the conclusion.

Example 3 — the stale regulation

Claude describes a regulation as it stood two years ago; it was amended last month. Suggestion: "use the newest model, it will know".

No model knows last month. Training cutoffs are hard boundaries. The fix is grounding: upload the amended text, or enable web search. Recognise the signal words — "last month", "current", "latest", "this week" — and reach for a source, not a tier.

The diagnostic question: does Claude lack information, or lack capability? Information problems — wrong names, stale facts, no access, forgotten rules — are fixed by surfaces and configuration, for free. Capability problems — shallow reasoning with the information already present — are fixed by tier or thinking, for money. Most problems are information problems.
Lesson 3.2

Choosing the surface: a need-by-need walkthrough

Why this matters. "Select appropriate features" is the first task statement. The exam gives you a need and four surfaces, usually including one that is heavier than necessary and one that cannot do the job at all.

The rule that decides most of these

Choose the simplest surface that fully meets the need. Heavier is not better; it is setup for no benefit, and it is the distractor.

Worked walkthrough — one analyst's week

Monday: "rewrite this paragraph in plainer English"

Chat. One-off, no context to persist, no deliverable to iterate. A Project or Artifact would be ceremony.

Tuesday: "every week I brief Claude on our product, audience and house style before I can get anything useful"

A Project. Persistent knowledge (product spec, style guide) plus custom instructions (audience, tone, rules), shared with the team. Memory might pick some of this up, but a Project guarantees it and applies it identically for everyone.

Wednesday: "draft a 12-page policy I will revise five or six times"

An Artifact, opened inside the Project. It renders in its own panel and is edited in place, so each round of revision does not regenerate twelve pages into the transcript. Inside the Project, the house style applies automatically.

Thursday morning: "what did the regulator announce this week?"

Web search. The model cannot know this week. With search enabled, the answer is grounded and cited; with it off, Claude answers from training data only.

Thursday afternoon: "compare five vendors using web sources plus our research folder on Google Drive, with citations"

Research mode with the Drive connector. Multi-source, multi-step, cited report. A single search cannot do this; a connector reaches the folder.

Friday: "go through the 40 contracts in this folder on my laptop and build a comparison spreadsheet"

Cowork. Many local files, autonomous multi-step work. Uploading forty PDFs to a Project would make them available for questions, but would not walk the folder and build the sheet.

Also Friday: "update the status on these Jira tickets from my notes"

A connector, with approval on each write. Live system access; a write is consequential, so it is gated. A CSV export cannot write, and screenshots do not scale.

Lesson 3.3

Choosing the tier, and managing context limits

Why this matters. "Differentiate models" and "manage context limitations" are both task statements. You need a repeatable way to choose a tier, an intuition for what moves cost, and a handful of techniques for large inputs and long sessions.

The four questions that choose a tier

1How hard is the reasoning? Multi-step, ambiguous, adversarial, long-horizon → up. Pattern-matching, extraction, routing → down.
2What does a wrong answer cost? This dominates. High-consequence work justifies the most capable model.
3What is the volume? A hundred a day: choose on quality. Ten million: the tier is the whole business case.
4Is anyone waiting? Interactive work favours faster models. Overnight work has no latency constraint at all.
The method: start at the tier your hardest requirement demands, then test whether a cheaper tier actually degrades the result on a sample you have checked by hand. Do not start cheap and hope; do not pay flagship prices for tagging.

Worked examples

Example 1 — three million invoices a month

Extract three fields from each. A hand-checked sample of 300 invoices shows Haiku, Sonnet and Opus get identical results. Choose Haiku. The sample is what makes this safe; paying more buys nothing. If a stakeholder wants "a safety margin", the answer is a bigger sample, not a bigger model.

Example 2 — the once-a-year decision

One acquisition agreement, high stakes, a week to read it. The most capable model, thinking on. Volume and latency are irrelevant; consequence is everything.

Example 3 — the mixed workload

50,000 support tickets a day; 3% are complex. Route: Haiku classifies all, escalates flagged cases to Sonnet or Opus. Whenever a scenario has volume and a hard minority, the split is the answer.

Managing context limitations — five techniques

1Send what is relevant, not what fits. Extract the sections that matter. A 1M window is headroom, not a target.
2Put reference material in a Project so it is available without being re-sent in every message.
3Start a fresh conversation with a short summary when a chat has grown long and drifts. Claude may compact automatically near the limit; a deliberate restart is cleaner.
4Decompose very large inputs: process in parts, then synthesise.
5Remember Haiku's window is 200K against the frontier tiers' 1M when a single document is huge.
Example 4 — the 900-page discovery bundle

A legal team wants themes across a 900-page bundle. Pasting it all into one chat on Haiku fails (window). On a frontier model it fits but the answer is diffuse. Better: split by document type, ask for themes per part with page references, then ask for a synthesis across the per-part outputs. Same work, better answers, and the per-part outputs are verifiable.

Lesson 4.1

Task fit: three conditions, all required

Why this matters. "Analyse use cases" means deciding where Claude belongs in a process and where it does not. The exam offers four tasks and asks which is the strongest or weakest fit. You need a test, not a feeling.

The idea

Beginners ask "what can AI do here?" and end up with demos. The consultant's question is narrower and more useful:

Where in this process does a human spend time on something that is text-shaped, repetitive, and checkable?

That third condition is the one people forget, and it is the one that matters most: a task nobody can evaluate is a task you cannot safely automate, however impressive the demo.

Lesson 4.2

Designing the workflow: six steps and a full worked example

Why this matters. "Support solution design" and "integrate into workflows" are task statements. The exam describes a process and asks what a sound redesign looks like — and it plants an undocumented step, a missing baseline, or a removed checkpoint for you to notice.

The six steps

1Map the current process honestly, including the informal steps. The undocumented "Priya always reads it before it goes out" step is usually the real quality control. Delete it and the redesign fails.
2Find the text-shaped bottleneck. Where does the time actually go? Often not where people say.
3Decide the split. What does Claude draft; what does the human decide? Write it down.
4Design the checkpoint. Who reviews what, against what standard, before it moves on. A workflow with no checkpoint is not a design.
5Define what good looks like, measurably, before building. Otherwise you cannot tell whether it worked.
6Pilot narrowly, measure against the baseline, then widen. One team, one document type, a few weeks.

Full worked example — RFP responses

Lesson 4.3

Communicating value and limitations honestly

Why this matters. A recurring question shape: an enthusiastic sponsor wants to announce something over-stated, or a sceptic wants to dismiss it. The right answer is never pure enthusiasm and never pure refusal. It quantifies, scopes, preserves accountability, and names the metric that could prove it wrong.

Worked examples — what they say, what you say

"It will replace the review step."

Reframe "It changes what review looks at — from writing to checking — and makes it faster. The step stays. In the pilot the reviewer's time per document went from 40 minutes to 12."

"It is right 95% of the time, so we can ship it."

Reframe "At 40,000 items a month, 5% is 2,000 wrong outputs. What does each one cost? Which cases fall in the 5% — are they random, or the difficult ones? And who catches them? Let's answer those before we ship."

"It has read all our documentation."

Reframe "It reads what we put in front of it, each time. If we want the documentation available in every conversation, we configure a Project; it does not retain it on its own."

"Let's roll it out to everyone next week."

Reframe "Two volunteers on familiar documents is weak evidence for four hundred people. Let's widen to a second team with a different document type, measure, and confirm training and a review standard are in place first."

"It gave the same answer twice, so it is reliable."

Reframe "Consistency is not accuracy. Two identical answers can be identically wrong. Reliability is measured against a checked sample."

"We expect a 90% efficiency gain."

Reframe "Retrieval and first-draft time falls from six days to about one; strategy and verification are unchanged and still owned by named people. Roughly nine days to four overall — and we will track win rate as well as cycle time, so we will know if quality slips."

Why the last reframe wins marks. It quantifies a specific stage rather than the whole process; it says what does not change; it keeps named accountability; and it names the metric that would show the change failed. An exam option with those four properties is the credible one.
Lesson 4.4

Non-determinism, downstream systems, and arithmetic

Why this matters. Three design facts trip up otherwise-sound workflows. The exam tests each.

Non-determinism

Ask the same question twice:

Run 1: "Approved — the request meets all three criteria."
Run 2: "This request is APPROVED. All criteria are satisfied."

Same meaning, different text. A downstream step that looked for the exact string "Approved — the request" breaks on run 2, intermittently, which is the worst kind of failure to find. Two design consequences: (1) if output feeds a system, constrain the format tightly, validate before consuming, and define what happens when validation fails; (2) a single good demo result is not evidence — you need repeated runs on representative inputs.

Reply with exactly one word: APPROVED or REJECTED. Nothing else.

And in the workflow: anything other than those two exact tokens goes to a human.

Arithmetic is not a language task

A board pack drafted by Claude includes a table whose totals do not add up. The instinct is to write a more careful prompt about the calculation rules. That still asks the model to do sums in its head.

Design Compute the figures in the finance system or a spreadsheet, where they can be audited; give Claude the verified numbers; let it write the narrative. If a calculation must happen inside the Claude workflow, that is what a code-execution tool is for — computing, not generating.

Where the big wins are

Prompt improvements make a step better. Structural changes remove steps. Removal always beats optimisation, which is why this domain carries 16% against prompting's 14%. When an exam option removes rework or moves a check earlier, it usually beats an option that words the prompt better.

Worked examples

Example 1 — the contradiction

The 2025 expense policy (limit £50 per meal) and the 2026 policy (limit £65) are both in knowledge. Asked the limit, Claude sometimes says £50, sometimes £65, sometimes both.

Tempting fixes that fail "Add an instruction: always use the newest information" — the model cannot reliably tell which is newer from the contents; dates may be absent or misleading. "Use a stronger model" — a better model will be more articulate about the wrong policy. "Ask users to say which year they mean" — pushes your data problem onto every user forever.

The fix Remove the 2025 policy. Version history belongs in your document management system, where it is auditable; the Project holds what is true now. Knowledge-base hygiene is a real answer and it is frequently the correct one.

Example 2 — the department-wide Project

A department head wants one Project with every document the department owns, shared with all 80 staff. Two problems: sensitivity boundaries collapse (the confidential client files are now available to everyone with access), and quality drops (contradictions, stale material, a sprawling base the model cannot navigate well). The right shape is several Projects, each scoped to a purpose and to who should see it.

Example 3 — the unowned Project

A shared Project set up a year ago has 400 files and no owner. Users report Claude "got worse". Nothing about the model changed; the configuration rotted. Maintenance means someone retires superseded files, updates instructions when the process changes, and checks the connected sources still point at the right places.

Writing instructions that work

Why this matters. "Create system instructions" is a task statement. The exam shows you instruction wordings and asks which is effective, or shows a symptom and asks which instruction fixes it.

Six rules, each with a before and after

1. Be specific about role and audience

Before "You are a helpful assistant."
After "You support the UK regulatory reporting team. Readers are compliance analysts, not lawyers."

2. State format defaults

Before "Be clear."
After "British English. Short paragraphs. Anything over 300 words starts with a three-bullet summary."

3. Phrase hard constraints as required actions

Before "Don't make mistakes about regulations."
After "Cite the document and section for every regulatory claim." A required action can be followed; a bare prohibition has no mechanism.

4. Give the escape hatch

Before "Always give a complete answer." (This pressures the model to fill gaps.)

5. Make mandatory documents mandatory

Before Uploading the naming rules and hoping.

6. Keep instructions short enough to maintain

Before 4,000 words including the style guide, the glossary and last year's priorities.

Worked examples

Example 1 — the personal account

A colleague wants to paste a client's unreleased financial results into their personal Claude account to draft a summary. "It is only a draft, and I'll delete the conversation afterwards."

Two independent problems. The classification: confidential, client-owned, possibly restricted by the client contract. The deployment: a personal consumer account is outside organisational control and consumer terms apply. A team must find patterns across 5,000 support tickets that contain names, emails and account numbers.

Pattern analysis rarely needs identities. Remove or mask the identifying fields first, then analyse. Note what does not work: analysing only 50 tickets reduces volume, not sensitivity, and cripples the analysis. Asking each customer for consent is disproportionate when anonymisation solves it.

Example 3 — enterprise versus consumer

"It is all Anthropic, so which account I use makes no difference." It does. Organisational (Team/Enterprise) and API usage carry stronger contractual guarantees, retention controls and administrative oversight, and business data is not used for training by default. Consumer accounts are governed by consumer terms. Which account and which deployment you use is itself a governance decision.

Lesson 6.3

Disclosure, and the two policy scenarios that recur

Why this matters. "Follow organisational policies" is a task statement, and two scenarios — the silent policy and the shadow tool — appear in some form on most papers.

When to say AI was involved

1Always when a policy or regulation requires it. Not a judgement call.
2When the recipient's decision would change if they knew. A customer who believes they have a personal reply from a named advisor.
3When you are being assessed on the work itself — academic submissions, certifications, competency evidence.
4Generally not required for routine internal drafting a human has reviewed, edited and owns — but follow your organisation's policy, which may set a stricter line.
Example — the customer letter

An AI-drafted letter is reviewed, edited and sent by a named advisor under their name: generally no disclosure needed, unless policy or regulation says otherwise. The same letter sent unedited, presented as a personal reply: the recipient's understanding would change if they knew, and human ownership is missing. Two problems, not one.

Scenario 1 — "the policy is silent"

A colleague argues that because the AI policy does not mention a use case involving customer personal data, it must be allowed.

Never "Proceed because it is not forbidden." Personal data carries legal obligations that exist independently of your internal policy; silence is not permission.

Do Escalate to whoever owns the policy and get a decision. If you must proceed in the meantime, take the conservative path: minimise the data, keep a human reviewing, do not publish externally.

Scenario 2 — shadow IT

A team is quietly getting excellent results from an unapproved AI tool on personal accounts.

Two wrong answers "Discipline them and block the tool" punishes users and leaves the need unmet, so it recurs less visibly. "Let them carry on, the results are good" accepts an ongoing data risk.

The right answer Bring the use case into the sanctioned environment. The value is real; the control gap is the problem. Options that preserve the value and close the gap score; options that do only one do not.

"Start a new conversation" is a real answer. In a very long chat the useful signal is buried among superseded discussion and abandoned attempts, and every turn re-processes all of it. Restating the current state cleanly is frequently the highest-leverage fix available.
Lesson 7.3

Optimisation, in the order to try it

Why this matters. "Optimise for efficiency and effectiveness". The exam tests whether you exhaust the free levers before trading quality, and whether you recognise that removing a human check is not an optimisation.
1Reduce the ask. Shorter outputs; no "explain your reasoning" you will not read; no extended thinking on easy tasks. Output tokens are the expensive kind.
2Reuse the setup. A Project stops the team re-pasting context; connectors stop re-uploading files.
3Right-size the model per step. Cheap tier for triage and extraction; capable tier only for the hard step. Measured.
4Batch what is not interactive. If nobody is waiting, do not pay interactive prices.
5Fix the process, not the prompt. The largest wins are structural.
Worked example — a costly report pipeline

A team generates 2,000 client reports a month. Each report: 8,000 words of output, on the top tier, with extended thinking, in a chat where the 90-page methodology document is pasted every time. Nobody reads past page two.

StepChangeQuality cost
1Cut to a two-page report with an appendix on requestNone — nobody read the rest
2Methodology document into a ProjectNone
3Turn off extended thinking; the task is templatedMeasure — likely none
4Try Sonnet on a checked sampleMeasure
5Remove the human review stepNot an optimisation. This removes a control.

Steps 1 and 2 typically cut cost by well over half with no quality change. Step 5 is the option the exam offers to see whether you will take it.

"Diagnose the layer before you spend. Layers 1 and 2 are free and account for most problems. Layer 3 costs money. Layer 4 means the task was never a language task."
