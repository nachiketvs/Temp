The three-altitude rule

The most important rule in this curriculum, and the reason the beginner material is safe to trust.

Every story block in every topic page describes the same true events at three altitudes. What changes between altitudes is the precision of the language — never the facts. Nothing taught at ground level has to be un-learned later.

Ground level — no jargon, but nothing false A truck full of corn pulls onto the scale at Elevator 7. It gets weighed full, tipped, and weighed empty. The difference is what the grower delivered. A slip of paper records it. Three hours later the network came back and the same slip got sent twice, so the co-op's records showed Dale delivering ninety-four loads when he'd delivered ninety.
Practitioner — the same events, named correctly The scale-house application is offline-capable and queues scale tickets locally. On reconnect it replays the queue, but the replay carries a local sequence number that resets on process restart, so it is not a stable idempotency key. The middleware's duplicate check reads a replica lagging fifteen minutes, so recently-ingested tickets appeared absent. 402 duplicate tickets were accepted.
Leader — the same events, as a control question An offline-first design created an at-least-once delivery guarantee that no downstream component was built to absorb. The duplicate control existed but was placed on an eventually-consistent read path, so it was decorative under exactly the load it was meant to protect against. The volume alert that should have caught it had been widened for harvest season. Three controls, all present, all defeated by peak conditions — which is when controls matter.
Why this matters for a beginner Notice that the ground-level version contains no falsehood and no toy analogy. There is no "think of the database as a filing cabinet." There is a real truck, a real slip of paper, and a real farmer who was paid the wrong amount. A beginner who only ever reads the first tier still has an accurate picture of what happened — they simply lack the vocabulary to name the parts. Adding vocabulary is additive. Un-teaching a bad analogy is not.

The constraint that shapes everything Harvest cannot be rescheduled. Corn is ready when it is ready. For roughly six weeks a year the business runs at more than twenty times its off-season transaction volume, and any system that fails during that window fails at the only moment the co-operative actually earns its money. Every architectural, testing and release decision in this curriculum can be traced back to that sentence.

2. Two directions of trade with the same people

Dale Whitcomb sells corn to the co-op and buys seed from the co-op. He is a supplier and a customer simultaneously, and the two relationships settle against each other.

2.1 Grain in — origination through settlement

This is the flow that earns the money, and the flow the incident breaks.

StepWhat physically happensWhat the system must record

Ground level A farmer promised to bring in a certain amount of corn at an agreed price. Every truckload gets weighed, tested for wetness, tipped out, and weighed again empty. The difference is what he brought. Once a week the co-op adds up his loads, subtracts what he owes for seed, and pays him the rest.
Practitioner A forward contract carries quantity, price basis and a delivery tolerance. Each scale ticket is a delivery event with a net weight derived from gross minus tare, adjusted by a moisture shrink factor. Tickets are applied against contract fill; quantity beyond tolerance is repriced at spot. Settlement aggregates applied tickets, applies discount schedules for quality, nets the member's input receivable, and produces a payment proposal.
Leader Origination-to-settlement is the co-operative's revenue-recognition and member-equity backbone. An error here is not a defect ticket — it is a restatement of what the business owes its owners. That is why this value stream carries the heaviest control requirement and the highest test-evidence bar in the programme.

2.2 Inputs out — the purchase already taught in ERP-CRM-1

The co-op buys seed, fertiliser and crop chemicals, holds them at the agronomy centres, and sells them to members — usually on credit, to be settled against the coming harvest. The 500-bag corn seed purchase from GreenGrow Supplies used throughout the SAP material sits here. It is deliberately the simpler of the two flows, which is why the curriculum starts there.

6. The September settlement gap

The narrative spine. One real, plausible failure that every discipline in this curriculum can interrogate from its own angle.

In one sentence During peak harvest, a three-hour network outage at one elevator caused 402 duplicate scale tickets to be accepted, crediting roughly 358,000 bushels of grain that was never delivered. Those phantom bushels pushed grower contracts past tolerance, repricing genuine deliveries at a lower spot price. The weekly settlement run then paid 340 growers on quantities that did not exist — about $1.8M of settlement value — and it went undetected for eleven days until a farmer phoned to say his own statement was too high.

6.1 How it unfolded

WhenWhat happened
Wed 24 Sep, 09:15A utility contractor cuts a fibre line near Ackley. Elevator 7 loses its network link. Trucks keep arriving — this is peak week.
09:15 – 12:20The scale-house app works exactly as designed: it keeps weighing and queues tickets locally. Renata never stops. 1,143 tickets accumulate in the local queue.
≈ 10:40The scale-house PC is restarted after a printer driver hangs. The local sequence counter resets to 1. Nobody notices; the queue survives the restart.
12:20Network restored. The app replays its queue.
12:20 – 12:26Middleware checks each incoming ticket against recent tickets to reject duplicates. That check reads a reporting replica lagging about 15 minutes. Tickets ingested just before the outage are not visible on it yet. 402 duplicates pass.
Wed – ThuDuplicates are applied against grower contracts. Contracts over-fill. Quantity beyond tolerance reprices at spot — that week $0.71/bushel below contract.
Thu 25 Sep, 02:00Weekly settlement run executes. 340 growers settled on wrong quantities — most overpaid for grain never delivered, some underpaid where repricing bit. About $1.8M of settlement value rests on phantom bushels.
Thu – SunVolume monitoring does not alert. Thresholds were widened for harvest; 402 extra tickets sits inside the seasonal noise band.
Mon 6 OctDale Whitcomb phones Priya: his statement shows 94 loads. He delivered 90. He knows because he keeps his own notebook.
6 – 9 OctReconciliation, correction run, member communications, and a very uncomfortable conversation with the board.

Note the direction of the error: most affected growers were overpaid, because they were credited for grain they never delivered. A minority were underpaid, where phantom volume pushed their real deliveries past contract tolerance and repriced them at spot. Both are wrong, and in a co-operative both are the members' own money.

Why a farmer reported being overpaid This surprises people, and it is the most realistic detail in the whole scenario. Dale is not a customer — he is an owner. Money the co-operative pays out incorrectly is money taken from the membership he belongs to, including himself. He also keeps a paper notebook of every load, as most growers do, because his income depends on it. So he had both the record and the motive to call.

For a tester, the lesson is sharper than the anecdote: the control that actually worked was a sixty-eight-year-old man with a notebook, and it took him eleven days. Every automated control you design is competing against that benchmark — and losing to it is worse than it sounds.

6.2 The three controls that all existed and all failed

Control-chain view

The controls were present; their assumptions were incompatible

A checklist would mark all three “implemented.” A journey test would show the holes lining up.

1 · Stable identityAssumed a local sequence uniquely represented a delivery. Restart reused the sequence.
2 · Duplicate rejectionAssumed the read copy was current. Replay arrived faster than replication.
3 · Abnormal-volume alertAssumed wider harvest thresholds still caught meaningful anomalies.
All three holes align under peak conditions402 duplicate tickets pass silently into contract application and settlement.
Professor's-margin note: test the assumptions between controls, not only whether each control returns its expected result in isolation.

1. Idempotency

Intended: replaying a ticket twice should be harmless. Failed because the key was a local sequence number that reset on restart — it identified a slot in a queue, not a delivery event.

2. Duplicate detection

Intended: middleware rejects tickets it has already seen. Failed because it read an eventually-consistent replica. Under normal load the lag was invisible. Under replay it was decisive.

3. Volume monitoring

Intended: an unusual ticket count raises an alert. Failed because harvest thresholds had been widened to stop the alert firing constantly — so it could no longer detect anything that mattered during harvest.

The lesson that generalises Every one of these controls was working correctly by its own specification. They failed together, under peak conditions, in a way no single-component test would reveal. This is the argument for integration-level and production-like testing that you will make repeatedly in interviews — and here you can make it with a story instead of a principle.

6.3 Why it is a good teaching incident

  • It is nobody's stupidity. Offline-first was a correct design for a scale house. Widening alerts during harvest was a reasonable operational decision. Reading a replica is standard practice. Good engineers made each choice.
  • It crosses every layer — client, queue, integration, ERP business rules, database consistency, monitoring, and human process.
  • It has a human ending. Not a dashboard, not an alert. A farmer with a notebook. That is what makes it memorable, and it is also true to how these things are usually found.
  • It has a money number. $1.8M and eleven days gives every improvement you propose a denominator.

10. The honesty boundary

Prairie Harvest Co-operative does not exist.

It is a teaching scaffold, built so that abstract concepts have somewhere concrete to live. It is deliberately realistic, which makes it deliberately dangerous in one specific way: a story this coherent is very easy to narrate as though you lived it.

You must never present Prairie Harvest, Harvest One, or the September settlement gap as your own experience. Not on a CV, not in an application, not as an answer to "tell me about a project you led," not as an example of something you personally fixed.

What it is legitimately for

  • Building intuition. Understanding idempotency through 402 duplicate tickets is faster and more durable than understanding it through a definition.
  • Rehearsing reasoning. "Here is how I would approach a duplicate-delivery problem" is a completely honest answer, and this world gives you somewhere to practise it.
  • Answering hypotheticals. When an interviewer says "imagine a system where..." you have a fully-furnished system ready.
  • Naming your real experience precisely. Once you can articulate what went wrong at Prairie Harvest, you will find you can finally articulate the equivalent thing you actually witnessed in twenty years of testing — which you had been describing vaguely because you lacked the vocabulary, not the experience.

The line, stated plainly

Use Prairie Harvest to explain how you think. Use your own twenty years to explain what you have done. Never let the first borrow the credibility of the second.
